01

“Who is on my ballot, and what have they actually done?”

Engine built · filling it

Election season is loud and almost none of it is useful. Posters, convoys, rented crowds. Almost nobody can tell you the name of the person running for their own state assembly seat, let alone what that person did the last time they held office.

Part of this already works. Every party has a page — who leads it, where its office is, which states it governs. And where your state has been switched on, you can already enter your location and see the races on your ballot.

What is missing is the candidates, and a way in. Seven people are on record for the 2027 presidential race, and exactly one of them has a photograph. For governor, in every state we checked, we have nobody at all yet. And there is still no page you can simply open and browse — the ballot appears only if your own state has been lit up.

The machine is built. What it needs now is names, faces, and a front door.
  • →One page listing every election we are tracking — national, governorship, assembly — open to anyone, not only your own state.
  • →Open a year and see every party and its campaign side by side.
  • →Tap a party to see its full slate — who it is fielding, in your state and everywhere else.
  • →Open a candidate and see their record attached — offices held, party history, and what the budget and corruption data already says about them.
  • →Compare two candidates in the same race, plainly, without the noise.
02

“Who is supposed to protect me, and how do I reach them?”

Next

When something happens, the questions are immediate and practical. Where is the nearest station? Who is the DPO here? If the division is the problem, who is above them? Most Nigerians cannot answer any of these for their own area, and the information is not written down anywhere a normal person can find it.

So we are building the security chain the same way we built the political one — from the top of your state all the way down to the division that actually covers your street.

Not a list of names. A chain you can walk up when the person below will not help you.
  • →Your state: the Commissioner of Police, the state Police Public Relations Officer, the Area Commanders — names, offices, and how to reach them.
  • →Your LGA: the divisions covering it and the Divisional Police Officer heading each one.
  • →Your ward: the actual stations near you, where they are, and their working numbers.
  • →Ranked from most senior down to nearest to you, so you know exactly who to escalate to.
  • →The same chain on every state, LGA and ward page you already use.
03

“What is actually in my ward?”

After that

Right now a ward is a name and a councillor. But nobody lives in a ward — people live in a community. And the things that decide whether a community is liveable are the clinic, the school, and whether either of them is real.

We are going one layer below the ward, then filling that layer with the public facilities that are supposed to be serving it. Public only. If your family pays for a private hospital or a private school, that is a market. What we are auditing is what the state promised you for free.

A ward with three schools on paper and one standing building is a story. You cannot tell it until both numbers exist.
  • →Find your community by name, not just your ward code.
  • →Every public hospital, health centre and clinic serving it — and the total for your whole LGA.
  • →Every public school — primary, secondary, and the universities in your state.
  • →Compare your LGA to the one next door. Same population, half the clinics? Now you can prove it.

This one needs you

There is no government file listing every community in Nigeria. There are 8,807 wards, and the people who know what is inside them are the people who live there.

When this opens, you will be able to add your own community, your own clinic, your own school — the same way people already add missing photos and phone numbers for their representatives today.

04

“What law is about to change my life?”

Later

Bills that reshape whole industries pass with almost nobody outside Abuja knowing they existed. The hearings are public — the National Assembly streams them live — but a six-hour stream nobody watches is not the same thing as transparency.

We are going to read those hearings so you do not have to, and then tell the people a bill actually affects that it is coming.

A bill about mining should reach the LGAs with mines in them before it passes, not after.
  • →Bills tracked as they move — what it does, in plain language, not legalese.
  • →Who proposed it and who is backing it — attached to the same profiles you already browse.
  • →Hearings streamed on the National Assembly channels, transcribed and summarised.
  • →If a bill hits your LGA, it shows up on your LGA page.
  • →Follow a bill and we tell you every time it moves, until it passes or dies.
05

“Who runs power, roads and schools — and where did the money go?”

Ongoing

This is the part we started with, and the part we never stop working on. We already hold hundreds of budget documents and hundreds of thousands of government payment records, and you can already ask questions about them in plain English or Pidgin.

What is missing is the human layer on top — the ministries that spend it, the people who run them, and the road from the federal purse all the way down to your local government.

  • →Every ministry that matters to daily life — power, works, education, health, agriculture — what it is responsible for and who heads it.
  • →How to reach each one, and what it currently claims to be working on.
  • →The money followed federal → state → local government, at every step.
  • →What your LGA received versus what got built.

Already working

None of this starts from zero.

The map underneath is already built and already public. Everything above is a new layer on ground that exists.

The honest part

Where you come in

Of the 2,847 officials we track, fewer than one in seven has a photograph — and the profiles behind those names are barely filled in. Two in every three are a name and nothing else. Almost nine in ten have no email, no phone number and no office address, so even when you find the right person there is no way to reach them. Exactly one official in the entire database has a complete profile.

We cannot fix that from a laptop in one city. There are 774 local governments and four of us.

So the fastest way any of this gets finished is you. Right now there is one thing you can help with, and it is the biggest gap on the list: identifying the public officials in your area. If you know your councillor’s face, your chairman’s office, a working number for your rep — you can add it today, and other people vote it up or down before it goes live.

Mapping the infrastructure — your community, your nearest station, the clinic and the school — opens next, and it works the same way. Officials first, because that is what is built.
