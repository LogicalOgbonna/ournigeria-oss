Where this goes next

The map of who governs Nigeria is built. Filling it is the work.

We have mapped 2,317 elective seats from governor down to ward councillor, across 8,807 wards and 774 local governments, and put it behind a plain-language interface in English and Pidgin. That structural work is done and it does not need doing twice. What follows are eight layers of accountability built on top of it — each one reusing the same spine, each one measurable.

01

The structure is finished. The record is thin.

It is worth being precise about where this actually stands, because the gap is not where most people assume. The engineering is not the constraint. The geography is resolved to ward level, the constituency mappings are in place, the personalised lookup works, and the retrieval stack over budget and spending data has been in production for months.

The constraint is that the records hanging off that structure are close to empty. Of 2,847 officials in the database, 13% have a photograph and 66% consist of a name and nothing else — no contact route, no biography, no party history a reader could check. The average profile has 2.0 of 10 tracked fields filled; exactly one official is complete. The election work is the sharpest illustration of the pattern: the ballot API, the sixteen-party directory and the location-to-races personaliser are all built and working, yet seven candidates are on record for the 2027 presidential race — one with a photograph — and none at all for governor in any state sampled. Beyond that there is no security layer, no facility layer and no legislative layer.

Every layer below is a data problem sitting on solved infrastructure — which is why they compound instead of competing.

Why eight layers cost less than eight products

04

Sequence and reasoning

First — Elections

The only time-bound layer on the list. A general election is a fixed date and a national attention spike; coverage published afterwards is worth a fraction of the same coverage published before.

It is also the cheapest layer to fill, because the candidate data is already collected and the seat structure it attaches to is already resolved.

Second — Security

The highest-frequency civic need on the list. People look up their representative occasionally; they look for a police division at the worst moment of their year.

It reuses the exact hierarchy pattern already built for elected officials — a jurisdiction, a role, a person, a term — so the engineering is largely a repeat.

Then — the ward interior

Communities, health facilities and schools are one problem wearing three hats. All three need a settlement layer beneath the ward, so communities must land first and the other two follow cheaply.

Legislation, ministries and budget depth then run continuously rather than as a phase.

06

The honest limits

A roadmap that lists only upside is not a roadmap. Four people cannot hand-verify 8,807 wards, and pretending otherwise is how civic data projects quietly die. The strategy assumes automation first, crowd verification second, and hand-curation reserved for the headline records only.

There are also constraints that money does not remove. Nigeria publishes no authoritative register of communities, and no public dataset of police divisions or their commanders. Health and education have partial public sources; security has effectively none, which is precisely why it is valuable and precisely why it will be slow.

The claim is not that every layer reaches completeness. It is that each one reaches a threshold where the pattern becomes visible and the gaps become arguments.

Committed

  • Election and party pages, with records attached for headline races
  • Full state-level command chain for security, division level where sourceable
  • Community mapping seeded by citizens, not hand-collected
  • Public health and education facilities where an open source exists

Explicitly not promised

  • Hand-verification of all 8,807 wards
  • Complete national coverage of police divisions in the first pass
  • Private hospitals or private schools — public provision only, by design
  • Real-time legislative coverage of every bill, rather than those with traceable local impact
